    Simulation of Nuclear Quantum Effects

    What you are looking at

    The animation at the top of the home page is a single water molecule: an oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. The nucleus of a hydrogen atom is a proton, and it is so light that it does not behave classically. Quantum chemistry makes it a small fuzzy cloud, showing where the proton is likely to be.

    The two white clouds are those protons. The red ball marks the oxygen. The glassy shell around everything is the cloud of electrons that holds the molecule together.

    The motion is a real simulation. We started the protons slightly pulled away from the oxygen, let them go, and computed step by step how the protons and electrons respond. (The method is called real-time NEO, short for nuclear–electronic orbital, and we ran it in ChronusQ, our group’s quantum chemistry program.) The two protons swing in and out together, the symmetric stretch familiar from infrared spectroscopy, and their clouds really do stretch and squeeze as they move. One swing takes about nine femtoseconds, nine millionths of a billionth of a second (1 fs = 10−15 s), so the animation is slowed down enormously.

    The quantum nature of the proton is easiest to see when a proton is shared between two molecules. In the small cluster below, one proton sits between two water molecules instead of belonging to either.

    A classical particle would have to sit on one side or the other. The quantum proton is one cloud shared by both molecules. In this simulation, computed with the same real-time NEO method, it swings back and forth between the two oxygens, each swing a few femtoseconds long. Hand-offs like this are how protons travel through water.

    From a single water to a nanodroplet

    Liquid water is many molecules bonded to their neighbours, and these quantum effects of the proton run through all of it. Treating every proton as a quantum cloud, rather than a point, brings the simulation closer to how water really behaves. On the home page, as you scroll, the camera pulls back and the single molecule becomes one of 250 in a small droplet. In that calculation all 500 protons were computed as quantum clouds at once.

    Making the calculation affordable

    A calculation like this grows steeply with size. Every electron and every quantum proton interacts with every other, and for the droplet the data describing all those interactions would not fit in any computer’s memory. Two things together made it possible: numerical techniques and high-performance computing. Most of that data is redundant, and a Cholesky decomposition keeps only the part that matters, orders of magnitude less to store. What remains runs in parallel on 52 nodes of the University of Washington’s Hyak cluster, about 10,000 processor cores working on the same droplet and exchanging results over the network (MPI). Both are built into ChronusQ.

    Want the details?

    The droplet calculation is described in our paper Nuclear–electronic orbital method at scale (APL Computational Physics, 2026; summary), which builds on our earlier work (J. Chem. Theory Comput., 2023; summary). For the method itself, see the review by Pavošević, Culpitt and Hammes-Schiffer (Chem. Rev., 2020) and the real-time NEO paper by Zhao et al. (J. Phys. Chem. Lett., 2020). The droplet geometry comes from Reddy et al. (J. Chem. Phys., 2017).

    All of the calculations were run with ChronusQ, the open-source program developed in Xiaosong Li’s group (Williams-Young et al., 2020).
